The Strategic Problem

The "Weekend-Only Wallet" Risk

JKOPay dominated QR payments at night markets and convenience stores — but users weren't opening the app for everyday payments. Leadership asked: how do we deepen daily-use frequency before a super-app competitor fills that gap?

⚠ Before: The Reality

High market share, low daily engagement

6M+ registered users. 50%+ market share in QR payments. But usage was concentrated at point-of-sale — night markets, convenience stores, weekend shopping. The app lived in users' "occasional use" mental model, not their daily routine. That made retention fragile and opened the door for super-app competitors to displace us entirely.

✦ The Strategic Goal

Expand into daily-use payment categories

Four categories where payment is a recurring, habitual need: bill pay (utilities, fines, government fees), taxi (daily commuting), fuel tax (annual sticky payment), charity (brand and engagement). If we could make JKOPay the app users reach for when a payment need arises — not just at checkout — the frequency problem solves itself.

⚡ The Constraint That Made This Hard

Taiwan's Electronic Payment Act required FSC regulator sign-off on each new payment category. Partner integrations needed to satisfy bank and government counterparties. The trust-account architecture (managing bank + 25 partner banks) had operational rigour we couldn't shortcut. The org's natural cadence: one new category every 6–9 months. My target: four categories in 12 months.

My PM Framework

Three Principles That Shaped Every Decision

I didn't treat this as four separate launches. I treated it as one product system with shared architecture, shared regulatory precedent, and a sequencing strategy designed to compress a 36-month roadmap into 12.

1

Sequence by regulatory friction — not revenue

The natural instinct is to launch the highest-revenue category first. In a regulated environment that's wrong. Each successful category approval creates a regulatory precedent that makes the next approval faster. Sequence the easiest approval first, accumulate precedent, then tackle the harder ones.

Lead with Charity (lowest friction) → build towards Taxi (most complex authorisation flow)
2

Parallelise approvals — never stack them

Default org behaviour: wait for one approval letter before filing the next submission. I broke this. File all four regulatory submissions in week one. Run a stop-light review every Friday with regulatory ops. Build engineering against the expected approval shape — accept some rework risk in exchange for 6 months of time-to-market compression.

Parallel regulatory tracks → shipped 4 categories in 12 months vs. org's natural 24–36 month pace
3

Design primitives — not point features

Every category launch produced one reusable payment primitive: the two-step auth/capture flow (built for taxi, reused by all future recurring-payment products), the recurring authorisation priority stack (fund-source fallback chain), and the idempotency key pattern across all four flows. Second and third product launches were dramatically cheaper to build.

The taxi two-step capture became the foundation for all subscription billing products that followed
Product Sequencing

Four Categories — One Deliberate Order

The order wasn't arbitrary. Each launch was chosen because of what it contributed to the next: regulatory precedent, technical primitives, or product trust with users.

First

🤝 Charity Donations (公益捐款)

Lowest Regulatory Friction
Why first: build regulatory precedent, brand halo, and team confidence with the simplest possible payment flow

Charity partners receive an API and QR code for donation collection. Users apply JKOPay coupons to donations — subsidised by JKOPay marketing budget — turning a simple feature into a brand-halo lever. No complex settlement requirements. No government counterparty reconciliation. Maximum learning value for minimum regulatory risk.

What it gave the next launch: FSC familiarity with JKOPay's payment category expansion pattern. Cleared the path for government-facing bill pay.

Second

🧾 Bill Pay (繳費)

Volume Engine
Why second: highest daily-frequency use case — utilities, fines, government fees. The single biggest driver of habitual engagement.

Integrated with Taiwan's government Omni-U Link 繳費API. Categories: utility bills, traffic fines, other government fees, health insurance contributions. User flow: scan barcode on physical bill → JKOPay parses payee and amount → confirm → pay via account balance, linked card, or stored value. Single-step capture (amount fixed by the bill itself).

Technical design: All settlement routed through the trust account at the managing bank + 25 partner-bank network. Idempotency key on every payment to protect against duplicate submission on the government reconciliation side.

📊 200% MAU growth within 2 weeks of launch
Third

⛽ Fuel Tax (燃料稅)

Sticky Recurring
Why third: piggybacks entirely on bill-pay infrastructure. Annual recurring payment = natural retention anchor.

Built on the bill-pay integration with a calendar reminder and one-tap repeat workflow. No new regulatory category required — fuel tax is a subset of the government-fee category already approved. Engineering cost was minimal (leveraged the bill-pay pipeline completely). Added a "pay again next year" save state, surfacing the product at the right annual moment.

Lesson learned: Adoption was lower than projected — most users paid fuel tax through their car-insurance broker rather than directly. Should have validated the user's existing workflow before committing to standalone product design.

Fourth

🚖 Taxi — Taiwan Taxi Co (叫車)

Two-Step Capture Flagship
Why last: most complex authorisation flow. Partnership with Taiwan's largest taxi fleet (台灣大車隊). Required building a new payment primitive from scratch.

Unlike the first three (fixed-amount capture), taxi required a pre-auth at trip start → capture at trip end flow because the final fare is only known when the trip ends. This meant designing the two-step capture as a generic primitive — not a taxi-only feature — with full idempotency, cancellation void paths, and fallback handling.

UX differentiation (vs Uber): Launched without Uber-parity map UI (would have taken 9–12 months). Shipped with two killer differentiators instead: fare estimation (Uber didn't offer reliably in Taiwan) + one-tap favourite trips. Won on functional differentiation; map UX caught up in the next two quarters.

🔧 Two-step capture became the recurring-payment primitive for all subscription products

Primitive 2: Recurring Auth Priority Stack

Configurable fund-source priority · Idempotency-safe across retries

When a user has multiple fund sources (account balance, linked card, stored value), which one gets debited first? Rather than hardcoding this, I designed a merchant-configurable priority stack with automatic fallback — so each payment partner could define their own preferred order, and the system cascades down if the primary source fails.

Transferable Insight

What This Translates to in European Fintech

The Core Pattern

In regulated environments, you ship at the speed of the slowest legal review. The only way to compress that is to parallelise approvals, not stack them — build engineering against the expected approval shape, run stop-light governance weekly, and accept some rework risk in exchange for time-to-market.

This is the same metabolism Wise uses for new payment corridor launches, Revolut used for its UK banking licence mobilisation (20 months with parallel product workstreams running throughout), and Checkout.com uses for new-market acquirer setups. The specific regulations differ; the operating discipline is identical.

And underneath the launches: design primitives, not point features. The two-step capture I built for taxi wasn't a taxi feature — it was a recurring-payment primitive that every subsequent product reused. In fintech, the products that scale fastest are the ones where each launch leaves behind a reusable layer for the next.
